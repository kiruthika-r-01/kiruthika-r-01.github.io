I am currently an unemployed content writer, which is a polite way of saying I spend my mornings begging robots to let me write about cloud software so I can buy groceries. Yesterday, a relentlessly cheerful recruiter with a title like "Talent Evangelist" asked me how I was monetizing my downtime. I told him I’d been sitting on my terrace watching crows yell at me and reading fourth-century BCE Chinese philosophy. He looked at me on Zoom like I had just confessed to arson. And so on.

The problem with being broke in the twenty-first century is that everybody expects you to turn your panic into a side hustle. If you like painting, you’re supposed to open an Instagram store. If you like thinking about the nature of existence, you’d better start a podcast and sell mattress sponsorships. Not everything I care about has to be useful, though. In fact, if everything I cared about had a practical application, I think I would probably walk straight into the Bay of Bengal.

A couple thousand years ago, the Taoist philosopher Zhuangzi wrote about a massive, gnarled oak tree that carpenters refused to chop down because its wood was completely worthless. Make a boat out of it, and the boat would sink. Make a coffin out of it, and the coffin would rot.

While all the straight, hard-working pear and cherry trees got sawed into lumber before their thirtieth birthdays, that ugly old oak lived for centuries, doing nothing except dropping acorns and shading stray dogs. If that tree were alive today, some guy in an OMR coworking space would write a LinkedIn post about how it lacks a growth mindset.

Kurt Vonnegut knew the secret of the useless tree. He loved telling the story about walking down the street to buy a single paper envelope. His wife pointed out that he could easily buy a hundred envelopes and stack them in the closet. Vonnegut pretended not to hear her. Why? Because walking to the newsstand meant he got to chat with the clerk, pet a basset hound, and watch a fire engine scream past. From a productivity standpoint, Kurt was a disaster. From a human standpoint, he was the only sane man in the room. We are here on Earth to fart around, he said, and no algorithm will ever forgive us for it.

Shortly before he died, Kurt told a class of high school kids to write a poem, make it as good as they could, show it to nobody, and rip it into tiny pieces. You don’t make things to get rich or famous. You do it to experience becoming, and to make your soul grow. My bank account currently has four hundred rupees in it, and I just spent three hours reading Kierkegaard instead of fixing my portfolio. It won’t get me hired. Still, my soul grew about half an inch before lunch. Hi ho.
